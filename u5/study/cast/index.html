Cast

6 actors and where each stood on 6 issues.

An actor here is a group that took part in the unit's story: a section of the country, the president, a community. First, who each one is. Then the issues, one at a time, with where each actor stood. A line marked Stretch is a loose fit: that actor took no clear stand on the issue, so the line gives the closest connection.

The 6 actors
ActorWho they were
The South Planters, Southern Democrats and Whigs; Calhoun, Davis, Hammond; yeoman farmers
The North Free Soilers and Republicans, merchants and mill owners, a loud abolitionist minority; Seward, Sumner, Lincoln
The West Settlers, miners and farmers of the Midwest, Kansas and California; Douglas
The President Polk, Taylor, Fillmore, Pierce, Buchanan, then Lincoln
Mexicans in the Cession Californios, Tejanos and Nuevomexicanos; Vallejo, Cortina
Immigrants Irish and German newcomers in Northern cities and Midwestern farms

Slavery in the territories

  • The South

    Slaveholders may take enslaved people into any territory (Calhoun, Dred Scott, Southern Democrats in 1860).

  • The North

    Free soil: keep slavery out of the territories (Wilmot Proviso, Republicans).

  • The West

    Popular sovereignty (Douglas); California chose free; Kansas fought over it.

  • The President

    Polk favored extending the 36°30′ line to the Pacific; Taylor wanted California admitted free; Fillmore signed 1850; Pierce and Buchanan backed proslavery Kansas.

  • Mexicans in the Cession

    Mexico had abolished slavery (1829); the Cession's fate was decided in Washington.

  • Immigrants

    Germans leaned free soil and Republican; the Irish leaned Democratic and distrusted abolitionists.

Fugitive slaves

  • The South

    Demanded a strict federal law (1850) and its enforcement in the North.

  • The North

    Many resisted the 1850 law (personal liberty laws, the Burns protests); others obeyed it to keep the Union.

  • The West

    Midwestern states such as Wisconsin and Ohio saw open defiance (Ableman v. Booth).

  • The President

    Fillmore and Pierce enforced the 1850 law, with federal troops in Boston in 1854.

  • Mexicans in the Cession

    Stretch Some enslaved people escaped from Texas to Mexico, which refused to return them.

  • Immigrants

    Stretch Irish workers often competed with free Black workers for the lowest-paid city jobs.

Expansion

  • The South

    For Texas and the Mexican Cession; many wanted Cuba (Ostend) and backed filibusters in Central America.

  • The North

    Many opposed the Mexican War and Cuba as plots to spread slavery, but backed Oregon and free homesteads.

  • The West

    Settlers pushed for Oregon, California and a railroad to the Pacific.

  • The President

    Polk took Texas, Oregon and the Southwest; Pierce bought the Gadsden strip and reached for Cuba.

  • Mexicans in the Cession

    Lost California and the Southwest; the treaty's promises of rights were often ignored.

  • Immigrants

    Many Germans settled the Midwest and central Texas.

Immigration and nativism

  • The South

    Few immigrants settled there, so nativism mattered less, though some former Whigs joined the Know-Nothings.

  • The North

    Took in most immigrants; nativist riots and the Know-Nothings were strongest here.

  • The West

    Germans farmed the Midwest; Chinese miners in California faced taxes and People v. Hall.

  • The President

    No federal action; immigration was left mostly to states and ports.

  • Mexicans in the Cession

    Treated as foreigners in their own land (Foreign Miners' Tax, California Land Act).

  • Immigrants

    The targets of nativism; built churches, schools and ethnic neighborhoods.

Parties

  • The South

    Southern Whigs collapsed; the Democrats became the South's party.

  • The North

    The Whigs collapsed; the Republicans became the North's majority party.

  • The West

    The Midwest swung from Douglas Democrats to the Republicans by 1860.

  • The President

    Pierce and Buchanan were Northern Democrats who leaned South.

  • Mexicans in the Cession

    Some Californios helped write California's 1849 constitution, but Anglo newcomers soon ran its politics.

  • Immigrants

    The Irish voted Democratic; Germans split, many becoming Republicans.

Union and secession

  • The South

    After Lincoln's election the Deep South seceded; the Upper South waited until Fort Sumter.

  • The North

    Secession was illegal; most Northerners would fight to keep the Union, not yet to end slavery.

  • The West

    The Midwest stayed with the Union; California and Oregon stayed loyal.

  • The President

    Buchanan said secession was illegal but that he could not stop it; Lincoln called the Union perpetual.

  • Mexicans in the Cession

    Stretch Tejanos fought on both sides of the Civil War.

  • Immigrants

    Large numbers served in the Union army (Unit 6).
