Traps

22 common mix-ups, each beside what is actually true.

  • Slaveholding in the South CED 4.13

    Trap

    Most white Southerners owned slaves.

    True

    About one in four white Southern families held enslaved people in 1860; most were non-slaveholding yeomen.

    Check

    CED KC-4.3.II.B.ii says so directly.

  • Compromise of 1850 CED 5.4

    Trap

    The Compromise of 1850 drew a new line like 36°30′.

    True

    It drew no line: California entered free, and Utah and New Mexico were organized without a ban on slavery.

    Check

    Look for “popular sovereignty,” not a latitude.

  • Kansas-Nebraska Act CED 5.6

    Trap

    The Kansas-Nebraska Act was another compromise.

    True

    It erased the Missouri Compromise line and blew up the old balance.

    Check

    Ask who gave up what. The North gave up the 1820 line and got nothing back.

  • Dred Scott v. Sandford CED 5.6

    Trap

    Dred Scott backed up popular sovereignty.

    True

    It said slave property could not be kept out of any territory, which undercut popular sovereignty; Douglas's Freeport Doctrine was a workaround.

    Check

    Who decides? Dred Scott answered: no one can bar slavery.

  • Republicans in 1860 CED 5.7

    Trap

    The Republicans of 1860 promised to abolish slavery.

    True

    They opposed slavery's spread into the territories; Lincoln promised not to touch it in the states.

    Check

    Free soil is not abolition.

  • Free Soilers CED 5.4

    Trap

    Free Soilers were abolitionists.

    True

    Many wanted the West kept for white farmers and workers, sometimes with no Black people at all.

    Check

    CED KC-5.2.I.A: some did not object to slavery on principle.

  • Abolitionists in the North CED 4.11

    Trap

    Most Northerners were abolitionists.

    True

    Abolitionists were a minority; Northern mobs attacked them too.

    Check

    Elijah Lovejoy (1837); the mob that dragged Garrison through Boston (1835).

  • Wilmot Proviso CED 5.3

    Trap

    The Wilmot Proviso became law.

    True

    It passed the House and died in the Senate.

    Check

    Its importance is the sectional vote, not a result.

  • Fugitive Slave Act of 1850 CED 5.4

    Trap

    The Fugitive Slave Act only affected people who had escaped.

    True

    It made Northerners liable to help, denied the accused a jury, and put free Black people at risk.

    Check

    Anthony Burns (1854); Solomon Northup's kidnapping shows the danger.

  • Uncle Tom's Cabin and the Underground Railroad CED 5.4

    Trap

    Uncle Tom's Cabin started the Underground Railroad.

    True

    The network had run for decades; the novel changed how Northerners felt.

    Check

    Check the dates: 1830s network, 1852 novel.

  • Nativists CED 5.5

    Trap

    Nativists were against all immigrants equally.

    True

    Their main target was Catholics, above all the Irish.

    Check

    CED KC-5.1.II.B: a strongly anti-Catholic movement.

  • Manifest Destiny CED 5.2

    Trap

    Manifest Destiny was a law or an official policy.

    True

    It was an idea, named by a newspaper editor in 1845; presidents and Congress acted on it.

    Check

    Ask who said it and where.

  • The war with Mexico CED 5.3

    Trap

    Everyone supported the war with Mexico.

    True

    Whigs like Lincoln, abolitionists like Douglass, and writers like Thoreau opposed it.

    Check

    The Spot Resolutions; “War with Mexico” (1848).

  • Seneca Falls Convention CED 4.11

    Trap

    Seneca Falls was only about the vote.

    True

    The Declaration of Sentiments listed many grievances: property, wages, education, church and divorce; the vote was the most debated.

    Check

    Read the list of grievances.

  • The South and industry CED 4.13

    Trap

    The South had no industry at all.

    True

    It had some, such as the Tredegar Iron Works, but far less than the North; profits went into land and enslaved people.

    Check

    Compare factories, railroads and immigrants, North and South.

  • States' rights CED 5.5

    Trap

    States' rights was the South's consistent principle.

    True

    Slaveholders demanded federal power to capture people who escaped; Northern states used states' rights against the Fugitive Slave Act.

    Check

    Ableman v. Booth (1859).

  • Secession after Lincoln's election CED 5.7

    Trap

    Every Southern state seceded right after Lincoln won.

    True

    Seven Deep South states left before his inauguration, four more after Fort Sumter, and four slave states never left.

    Check

    Two waves, plus the border states.

  • Dred Scott's case CED 5.6

    Trap

    Dred Scott won his freedom in court.

    True

    The Court ruled against him; his owners' family freed him later in 1857.

    Check

    Read the outcome, not just the issue.

  • Lincoln in 1860 CED 5.7

    Trap

    Lincoln campaigned everywhere in 1860.

    True

    He was not on the ballot in ten slave states.

    Check

    Look at the electoral map.

  • Second Great Awakening CED 4.10

    Trap

    The Second Great Awakening turned people away from religion.

    True

    It was a Protestant revival; it turned away from rationalism and Deism.

    Check

    Camp meetings and Finney's revivals.

  • Reform movements CED 4.11

    Trap

    Reform movements were run by the government.

    True

    Most reform worked through voluntary organizations; laws such as the Maine Law and new school boards came later.

    Check

    CED KC-4.1.III: mostly outside of government.

  • Mexicans in the Cession CED 5.3

    Trap

    Mexicans in the Mexican Cession were denied citizenship.

    True

    The treaty offered citizenship, but many lost land and rights in practice.

    Check

    California Land Act (1851); Vallejo's memoir.
