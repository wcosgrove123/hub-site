Chains

Each effect becomes the next cause.

Read a chain top to bottom, then try it scrambled in Chain Reaction.

A Expansion reopens the slavery question

  1. Annexation of Texas 1845

    Mexico still claimed Texas, and the two countries disagreed on its border.

  2. Polk sends Taylor's army to the Rio Grande 1846

    Troops in the land both countries claimed led to fighting (the Thornton Affair).

  3. Mexican–American War 1846–48

    Winning new land raised the question of whether slavery could go there.

  4. Wilmot Proviso 1846

    The House vote split both parties by section, which gave rise to free soil politics.

  5. Mexican Cession (Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo) 1848

    Turned the question into a real territory that had to be organized.

  6. California Gold Rush 1848–49

    California filled with people fast and asked to skip the territory stage as a free state.

  7. Crisis over California 1850

    A free California would tip the Senate, so Southern leaders demanded something in return.

  8. Compromise of 1850 1850

    Its price for the South was a strict new Fugitive Slave Act.

  9. Fugitive Slave Act 1850

    Forced Northerners to take part in slavery, which turned many against it.

  10. Uncle Tom's Cabin, personal liberty laws, Anthony Burns 1852–54

B From popular sovereignty to secession

  1. Race for a Pacific railroad route 1853

    Douglas wanted a central route, which needed the Nebraska country organized.

  2. Kansas-Nebraska Act 1854

    Repealed the Missouri line and left slavery to a vote of the settlers.

  3. Whigs collapse; Republicans and Know-Nothings rise 1854–55

    The old two-party system broke along sectional lines.

  4. Bleeding Kansas 1855–56

    Both sides sent settlers and guns; votes were stolen and towns attacked.

  5. Caning of Charles Sumner 1856

    Violence reached the Senate floor, and trust between sectional leaders broke down.

  6. Election of 1856 1856

    Republicans carried 11 Northern states in their first race; Buchanan won with Southern votes.

  7. Dred Scott v. Sandford 1857

    Said Congress could not ban slavery in any territory, so the Republican program seemed unconstitutional.

  8. Lecompton Constitution 1857–58

    Buchanan backed it and Douglas fought it, splitting the Democrats.

  9. Lincoln–Douglas debates (House Divided, Freeport Doctrine) 1858

    Douglas's answer to Dred Scott cost him the South; Lincoln became a national leader.

  10. Harpers Ferry raid 1859

    Northern sympathy for John Brown convinced many white Southerners that the North meant to destroy them.

  11. Democratic split 1860

    With two Democratic candidates, the Republicans could win with Northern votes alone.

  12. Election of Lincoln 1860

    A president elected with no Southern electoral votes looked to the Deep South like a threat to slavery.

  13. South Carolina and the Deep South secede 1860–61

    Seven states formed the Confederacy and seized federal forts.

  14. Fort Sumter 1861

C Abolition radicalizes, the South hardens

  1. Gradual emancipation and the American Colonization Society 1780–1816

    Early antislavery was slow, or meant removing free Black Americans.

  2. Walker's Appeal and Freedom's Journal 1827–29

    Black abolitionists rejected colonization and demanded freedom now.

  3. The Liberator and immediatism 1831

    White abolitionists like Garrison took up immediate emancipation.

  4. Nat Turner's Rebellion 1831

    White Southerners blamed abolitionist writing and feared more revolts.

  5. Virginia debate rejects emancipation 1832

    The South closed off debate about ending slavery.

  6. Dew and Calhoun: slavery as a positive good 1832–37

    Defenders moved from apology to open celebration of slavery.

  7. Mail burning and the gag rule 1835–36

    Silencing abolitionists made the fight about free speech and petition too.

  8. Liberty Party 1840

    Some abolitionists turned to elections.

  9. Free Soil Party 1848

    Stopping slavery's spread drew far more voters than abolition did.

  10. Republican Party 1854

D Revival to reform to women's rights

  1. Market revolution and a reaction to rationalism 1800–1830s

    Rapid change and mobility left people searching for meaning and order.

  2. Second Great Awakening 1800–1840s

    Preachers taught that people could choose salvation and should perfect society.

  3. Voluntary organizations 1810s–30s

    Converts organized societies to change behavior and institutions.

  4. Temperance, schools, asylums and abolition 1820s–40s

    Women did much of the work, gaining skills in speaking, writing and petitioning.

  5. Grimké sisters attacked for speaking in public 1837

    Defending their right to speak made them argue for women's rights.

  6. World Anti-Slavery Convention shuts out women 1840

    Mott and Stanton met and resolved to hold a convention for women's rights.

  7. Seneca Falls and the Declaration of Sentiments 1848

    Put women's demands, including the vote, on record.

  8. New York property law; Stanton and Anthony 1848–51

E Cotton builds a section

  1. Cotton gin 1793

    Made short-staple cotton profitable across the upland South.

  2. British and Northern textile mills 1810s–50s

    Rising demand made cotton the nation's leading export.

  3. Soil exhaustion in the Southeast 1820s–40s

    Worn-out land pushed planters to fresher soil in the West.

  4. Internal slave trade and the move west 1820s–50s

    About a million enslaved people were sold or moved to the cotton Southwest.

  5. King Cotton and little industry 1840s–50s

    Profits stayed in land and slaves, so the South built few factories and drew few immigrants.

  6. Helper's The Impending Crisis 1857

    A Southern critic blamed slavery for the South's lag; the South banned the book.

  7. “Cotton is king” and secession 1858–61

F Immigration and the collapse of the parties

  1. Irish famine and German revolutions 1845–48

    Millions of Europeans were pushed to leave home.

  2. Immigrants crowd Northern cities 1845–55

    Most were Catholic and many voted Democratic.

  3. School Bible fights and the Philadelphia riots 1840–44

    Protestant-Catholic conflict turned violent and political.

  4. Know-Nothing Party 1854–56

    Nativist voters left the Whigs and Democrats.

  5. Whig collapse 1854–56

    Northern voters looked for a new home.

  6. Republicans absorb free soilers and nativists 1856

G The people of the new lands

  1. Mexican–American War 1846–48

    The U.S. took land where Mexican and Native peoples lived.

  2. Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo 1848

    Promised citizenship and property rights to Mexicans who stayed.

  3. Gold Rush 1848–55

    Newcomers poured onto Californio land and Native land.

  4. Foreign Miners' Tax and California Land Act 1850–51

    Laws pushed Mexican, Latin American and Chinese miners out and made Californios prove their land titles.

  5. People v. Hall 1854

    Chinese immigrants lost the right to testify against white people.

  6. Treaty of Fort Laramie and reservations 1851

    Native nations were confined to bounded lands as settlers moved west.

  7. Cortina's revolt 1859
