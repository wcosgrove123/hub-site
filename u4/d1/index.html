The Elections of 1824 and 1828

Question of the day · PCE

What makes an election legitimate?

Underneath, the unit question: what can a majority do, and who can stop it? 1824: the most votes lose in the House. 1828: the most votes win and call it the people's victory.

The question's shape: causation.

Warm-up 7 min

Pre-thesis, collected and kept until 11/4. Stimulus: the 1824 table from 10/12 (Jackson 99 electoral votes and the most popular votes, Adams 84, Crawford 41, Clay 37; the House picks Adams). Prompt in AP wording: Evaluate the extent to which the expansion of participatory democracy changed American politics from 1800 to 1848. One sentence with the formula. Not graded; this is the before.

  • Construct a first thesis on the unit prompt with a counterclaim, a claim with an extent, and two reasons.
  • Prepare one region's position on an assigned debate topic with one piece of specific evidence (CED 4.3).

Contextualization 3 min

Model the context sentence with the QOTD as the prompt. Before: most states drop property requirements between 1800 and the 1820s; the Panic of 1819 turns farmers and workers against banks; Missouri splits the sections. During: one party, four candidates, a caucus nobody respects. After: two new parties.

Content 12 min

Five slides. 1 The corrupt bargain in one slide (recap from 10/12: 12th Amendment, top three, Clay as Speaker and then secretary of state). 2 Adams vs. Jackson: what each could not stand about the other (the split-box slide outlined 10/11). 3 How they showed it: the mudslinging of 1828, each charge beside the record (outlined 10/11 with period quotes). 4 Democratization: property requirements gone in most states, electors chosen by voters, turnout more than doubles from 1824 to 1828, Jackson 178 to Adams 83; who is still outside (women, Native nations, enslaved people, and free Black men in most states). 5 The new rules: King Caucus to nominating conventions (1831–1832), rotation in office and the spoils system.

Activity 15 min

Sectionalism debate prep (dept sheet). Eight pods become four regions, two pods each: North with Webster, South with Calhoun, West with Clay, National with Jackson. Inside a region: split the seven topics (slavery, transportation, industry vs. agriculture, states' rights vs. federal power, Tariff of 1828, Indian policy, national bank), pick a speaker, assign two question writers. Each student leaves with one topic, one position, and one piece of specific evidence. The department's Age of Democracy reading opens this block if it is a page or less; otherwise it goes home.

Closure 4 min

Exit sentence on the QOTD: one reason 1828 looked more legitimate than 1824 to Jackson's voters, and one group it did not include. Regions confirm their speaker.
